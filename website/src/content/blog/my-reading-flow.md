I am a perfectionist. It's a horrible quality. I've always struggled with finding the _perfect_ way to learn. I have this innate desire to know, fundamentally and from first principles, how everything works. When it works, it works great -- I remember never memorizing or bringing cheat sheets to my IB physics tests. I used to derive centripetal force from Newton's laws, or Lenz's Law from the basics of electromagnetism. It gave (still gives) me a sort of thrill, albeit a fake one, of 'discovering' something.

I refuse to move on to the next chapter if I don't completely understand everything about the current one. If an explanation doesn't make sense to me, there's an almost palpable feeling of unease I tend to feel. My brain is going:

> I don't want to have shaky fundamentals

So, usually, the story ends there. I refuse. I don't move on. I fall behind. And eventually, I either drop the subject because I couldn't do it perfectly, or I race against time to cram it all for a final. Every single time in that second scenario, all I wish for is one more day. Probably sounds familiar to a lot of university students.

When I first met Dr. Fok-Sheun Leung, I asked him what he thought about that. At the time, I was taking MATH 223 and 226 at UBC (honours linear algebra and calculus 3, respectively). It was near the start of the term, but I was beginning to feel my grip loosen. He told me, "you absolute fool, that's not how learning works" (paraphrased).

Of course, I knew this. I knew this every single time I started cramming and realized "wait, that concept I was stuck on kinda makes sense now". Thinking of learning as climbing a set of stairs is flawed. And I should know this. When I read _The Educated Imagination_ back in IB English, what fascinated me was this 'web of ideas' that humanity builds upon. Imagine trying to make a web of ideas, sequentially, perfectly. Good luck.

---

When I first started annotating my books (which I used to think is sacriligeous), I was all alone in my first co-op in Victoria. I had lots of time to read. It was uncomfortable at first, but soon I understood the flow.

My reading habit was sadly short-lived. When I did come back to those books later, I realized something fascinating. There was a significant number of ocurrenaces I would have of reading some paragraph, having a thought about it I wanted to write down, and noticing that Victorian-era * Aaryan had already scribbled it down. Over and over, I would see that the exact same *aha\* moment I had, had already been had. It was like doing a race with ghost mode activated; every step, every turn, I could feel the shadow of my thoughts.

The books I was reading at the time had a massive impact on the way I _thought_, my _being_. If I could have a system where I could read something, leave my thoughts imprinted upon the words, and an easy way to search through those thoughts, I could dissect my opinions from this rational set of axioms I have constructed. If I ever have a decision to make where I just **can't** find the right answer, I could come back to these axioms. That's not to say I become a rigid machine of rationality. If, for some weird gut-related reason, I just feel _different_ about this problem, disagree with what I should theoretically do, that doesn't make this system fall apart. It just adds further data into who I am. I would just extend these axioms with another, all while making sure the system remains consistent. (Ray Dalio seems to have had the same idea.)

Right off the bat, I knew physically writing on these books wasn't going to cut it (hear that, that's my heart pumping for joy knowing I don't have to defile these books again). Lots to be said about the **many** benefits of physically reading. None to be said here.

I started looking. Honestly, not for long. The first thing I saw was Reader. It was amazing. Stop the search, I have found it. It's an app which can convert any article you happen upon into highlightable text with the ability to attach notes. Not to mention, it includes it's own homepage where it renders the article in an extremely clean, distraction-free UX. On top of that, it is AI-native. You can 'chat' with your highlights. This was huge for me. Gone were the days of forgetting what I talked about. Context switching to understand a concept. I could just highlight the text, press `G`, and start relaying my questions to a model who knew exactly what I am reading and where I was at. If this sounds like an ad for Reader, that's not far from the purpose. It is genuinely a great app.
